Memory Under Interference:
Context Utilization, Speciﬁcation Confusion, and Inference Eﬃciency
in Small Code Language Models
Abstract
Increasing a language model’s context makes more
information available, but also adds computational
work and potentially competing instructions. When
does that extra information help a model solve the
intended task? I investigate this question through
seven studies containing 5,528 experimental requests
to quantized Qwen2.5-Coder 0.5B and 1.5B models
running on a CPU laptop. The experiments vary task
descriptions, competing requirements, interruptions,
retained context and the placement of a code-only
preference. In addition to testing the requested so-
lution, I test whether an incorrect answer satisﬁes
a competing requirement. This posthoc comparison
ﬁnds 8.97 percentage points more such failures un-
der competing than irrelevant context on 1.5B (95%
task-bootstrap interval [3.21, 16.67]). The 0.5B com-
parison is inconclusive, and conservative assumptions
about incomplete generations weaken the 1.5B re-
sult. Retaining context using known relevance labels
yields observed estimated net CPU-package energy-
per-correct-solution reductions of 76.5% and 67.4%
for 0.5B and 1.5B relative to full history. Accuracy
preservation remains uncertain, and diﬀerences in
prompt length, position, instructions and generated
output confound attribution of these reductions to re-
tention alone. The results motivate evaluating mem-
ory management through the answers it produces
and its total computational cost, alongside the rele-
vance of the information it supplies.
1 Introduction
A coding assistant often receives more than the task
it must solve. Its context may contain earlier re-
quests, examples, preferences and descriptions of re-
lated problems. Keeping that history can preserve
useful information. It can also leave an old require-
ment beside a new one, increasing both the amount
of text to process and the chance of producing a
plausible answer to the wrong problem. The cen-
tral question is whether the model uses the informa-
tion needed for the current task, rather than simply
whether that information ﬁts in its context.
This question grew out of Prompt-Energy, my ear-
lier experiment on how prompt design relates to code
correctness, token use, latency and estimated infer-
ence energy. Its implementation and report showed
me why response length and the cost of unsuccessful
attempts belong in an evaluation of prompting. The
present study asks a broader question: what happens
when context accumulates, and useful instructions
appear alongside irrelevant or competing informa-
tion? Prompt-Energy motivated this work, but its
earlier observations are a separate dataset and are
not pooled with the experiments reported here.
Existing research shows that context position mat-
ters, that conversation history can interfere with a
new task, and that preferences become diﬃcult to fol-
low in long interactions [ 4, 7, 15]. Other work ﬁnds
beneﬁts from combining tasks [ 11]. More context
is therefore neither inherently useful nor inherently
harmful. I examine when its content matters for two
small local code models, using executable tests to dis-
tinguish the requested behavior from a competing
behavior.
Three questions guide the experiments. RQ1: Does
additional task content change correctness compared
with similarly long neutral text? RQ2: When an an-
swer is wrong, does it satisfy a competing require-
ment present in context? RQ3: How do context-
retention policies and preference instructions relate
to correctness and computational cost?
The contribution is a set of paired code-task com-
parisons with inspectable prompts and outputs, a
cross-speciﬁcation diagnosis of failures, and an evalu-
ation of cost per correct solution that includes failed
attempts. The comparisons reveal a mixed picture:
the larger model’s error distribution changes in the
complete cases, reduced context has lower observed
estimated cost, and several accuracy and reminder
comparisons remain uncertain. The posthoc analy-
sis and its sensitivity to incomplete generations are
reported alongside the positive ﬁndings.
These results lead naturally to selective context man-
agement. To explore that practical direction, I de-
veloped MemoryGraph, a local prototype for stor-
ing, organizing and retrieving historical information
before it enters a prompt. It combines lexical and
embedding retrieval with a small entity graph and
lets the user inspect and approve selected memories.
Its separate synthetic evaluation measures ranking;
downstream correctness and energy beneﬁts remain
untested. I describe the existing prototype after the
context experiments, connecting the measurement
questions to a system that could be evaluated in sub-
sequent work.

2 Related Work
Using context and resolving instructions. Lost
in the Middle shows that the position of relevant ma-
terial can change performance in question answer-
ing and retrieval [ 7]. Gupta et al. study interference
when conversational history switches tasks [ 4]. Son
et al. examine following several instructions in one
call and ﬁnd that combining tasks can improve some
outcomes [ 11]. These studies motivate comparisons
that vary content as well as length. My interruption
experiment uses constructed transcripts rather than
actual earlier model execution, and my task bundles
use smaller code models and diﬀerent workloads. In-
struction Hierarchy studies training models to respect
instruction priorities [ 12]; here I evaluate ﬁxed mod-
els without training an instruction hierarchy or test-
ing adversarial security robustness.
Preferences and memory systems. PrefEval
examines explicit and implicit preference following
across longer conversations [ 15]. My code-only pref-
erence is a narrower case, with a scoring limitation
discussed in the results. LongMemEval separates
indexing, retrieval and reading [ 13]; EvalMem diag-
noses encoding, retrieval and generation failures [ 8].
LongMemEval-V2 extends memory evaluation to
accumulated experience in agent environments [ 14].
This work builds on the distinction between supply-
ing information and using it, rather than claiming
that distinction as new. The main experiments be-
gin with already assembled prompts, so they examine
code generation from available context rather than
the quality of a memory store.
Code correctness and computational cost. Hu-
manEval evaluates generated functions through ex-
ecution [ 1]. EvalPlus adds tests and demonstrates
that limited test coverage can change both mea-
sured correctness and model rankings [ 6]. I use its
expanded benchmark with the Qwen2.5-Coder fam-
ily [ 5]. Inference-energy research links cost to output
length, prompts and runtime choices [ 10]; a directly
related 2026 study examines prompting, code accu-
racy and environmental cost across small and open
models [ 9]. The present measurements concern CPU-
package energy on one laptop, without transferring
carbon or hardware conclusions from those studies.
Retrieving useful history . GraphRAG uses
graph indexes and community summaries for ques-
tions about a corpus [ 3]; Mem0 evaluates a more inte-
grated persistent-memory system [ 2]. MemoryGraph
is a smaller companion prototype with local mem-
ory editing, ranking and a basic entity graph. Its role
here is to identify a practical next evaluation, not to
claim the architecture or demonstrated beneﬁts of
these systems. The accompanying related-work table
records the overlap and distinctions in this focused
literature review; it is not a systematic search or a
claim of priority.
3 Evaluation Framework
The experiments separate four ideas. Availability
means that the needed speciﬁcation is present in the
prompt. Retrieval is the preceding act of selecting
stored material, illustrated separately by Memory-
Graph. Utilization is measured through what the gen-
erated function does on its tests. Eﬃciency concerns
tokens, latency and estimated CPU-package energy.
These observable outcomes do not measure internal
memory capacity or cognitive states.
An incorrect solution can still be informative. Sup-
pose the target requires a strict comparison, while
a background task includes equality. A solution us-
ing the inclusive rule is wrong for the target but may
be right for that background task. I test each com-
plete response against target suite T and alternative
suite A, distinguishing target-only pass, alternative-
only pass, both pass and both fail. An alternative-
consistent failure is thus an incorrect target solution
that satisﬁes the competing computational require-
ment. “Speciﬁcation confusion” names this observed
pattern, not a diagnosis of the model’s internal rea-
soning. Finite tests, extraction errors and ordinary
algorithmic mistakes remain relevant to interpreting
every cell.

4.2 What the context comparisons
test
To separate reading extra tasks from solving them,
passive load adds other task descriptions, while
active load requests their functions as well. K ∈
{2, 3, 4, 6}; neutral ﬁller matches passive input length.
Target placement and companions are ﬁxed. Ac-
tive requests do more output work, so focal-target,
per-function and whole-bundle endpoints are kept
distinct. Reused component-isolation outputs are
lookup references, not newly measured bundle exe-
cution.
To test whether a competing requirement changes the
answer, the revised interference experiment crosses
three ﬁller types, ﬁller-token targets 1,000/2,000 and
target beginning/end, with matched isolation con-
trols. Competing descriptions change a computa-
tional requirement, such as strict versus non-strict
comparison or inclusive versus exclusive boundaries.
Alternative references and distinguishing inputs were
validated. The model sees descriptions, not the alter-
native implementation or hidden tests. Related/irrel-
evant labels are authored coarse categories; they are
not calibrated semantic similarity. Filler budgets do
not guarantee identical complete input lengths across
every wrapper, an important limit on attributing dif-
ferences solely to content.
To examine the cost of keeping history, I compare
full history, target/query only, verbatim target replay,
lossless target-format compaction, and oracle reten-
tion. Oracle retention means selecting context using
known relevance labels: the target speciﬁcation and
preference are kept without having to discover which
history is relevant. Eight ﬁxed distractor descriptions
form full history. No-history omits the explicit code-
only preference, while the oracle retains target, pref-
erence and ﬁnal query. Compaction removes redun-
dant target formatting and drops other history; it is
not semantic summarization of a conversation. These
policies jointly change length, position, repetition and
sometimes instruction content.
To examine returning to a task after intervening de-
scriptions, the static interruption conditions contain
neutral material, one similar competing description,

4.3 Statistics, exclusions and energy
Intervals use 2,000 paired task-bootstrap draws with
seed 1732. Binary pairs use exact discordant-pair Mc-
Nemar tests; task means over repeated cells use exact
paired sign swaps. Holm correction is within speciﬁed
families: two isolated strategy tests, two initial-load
primary tests, two revised-interference primary tests,
six retention tests and six interruption tests. The
new cross-pass audit uses four posthoc tests. These
corrections do not control every analysis ever exam-
ined in the project. Intervals are pointwise, not simul-
taneous family intervals. Null tests do not establish
equivalent accuracy.
I preserve failed requests and capped generations.
Caps are nonpasses for bounded correctness end-
points but unknown for the uncensored cross-pass
taxonomy. API, input or evaluator-infrastructure fail-
ures are missing for valid-input accuracy and sepa-
rately reduce operational yield. I report cap exclu-
sion, optimistic bounded-correctness scenarios, ad-
verse cross-pass coding, independently labelled el-
igibility subsets, and exclusion of the documented
HumanEval/32 oracle anomaly. The expanded-test
adapter and sandbox restrictions remain part of the
operational outcome, not an infallible correctness ora-
cle.
HWiNFO CPU Package Power was logged at a nomi-
nal 500 ms interval. Gross energy uses trapezoidal in-
tegration; net estimates subtract the block-start idle
baseline over the request window. Approximate post-
load and total windows are both retained. End/mid-
point subtraction, drift exclusions and short-window
exclusions are secondary sensitivities. Per-condition
totals are preferred to precise interpretations of short
individual windows. The registered primary cache-
energy experiment is incomplete; retention energy
remains descriptive or posthoc. No whole-system elec-
tricity, carbon, GPU or cloud inference is measured.
5 Contextual Interference
5.1 Does task content matter beyond
length?
The task-bundle experiment asks whether similarly
long contexts behave diﬀerently when the extra text
describes other problems. Figure 2 compares correct-
ness of the designated target function under passive,
active and neutral conditions. Passive-minus-ﬁller
estimates tend to be negative across K in this ros-
ter, but many adjusted comparisons are inconclu-
sive. The 0.5B K = 6 estimate is −25 percentage
points, conditional interval [−37.5, −12.5], with Holm
p = 0 .015625 in its eight-comparison posthoc family.
This is evidence about the ﬁxed task combinations
tested here, rather than a general penalty for multi-
tasking.
Tasks recur in several bundles, and all bundles are
connected through shared members. The bootstrap
therefore describes variation across target tasks while
keeping their companions ﬁxed; it cannot supply
uncertainty for independently resampled bundles.
Changing K also changes which companion functions
appear. Active per-function fractions and whole-
bundle successes are reported against the same com-
ponents’ isolation outcomes in the archive, rather
than equating a passive focal pass with a complete
active bundle. The additional output budget and re-
peated component references limit cost comparisons.
The shared companions and additional output work
are important alternative explanations when compar-
ing bundle performance and cost.
5.2 Does a wrong answer follow the
competing requirement?
The ﬁrst comparison asks whether competing descrip-
tions reduce target correctness relative to irrelevant
descriptions. The registered diﬀerence is -11.25 per-
centage points for 0.5B, interval [-20.62, -2.50], Holm
p = 0 .056396, and -2.50 for 1.5B, interval [-10.00,
4.39], Holm p = 0 .63525. Both comparisons have
40 paired tasks averaged over four length/position
cells. A pointwise interval excluding zero can coexist
with an adjusted test that does not reject; both are
reported. Figure 3 shows the cell-level outcomes with-
out treating them as independent task replications.
Overall accuracy does not describe which requirement
a wrong answer follows. Testing the same saved pro-
grams against the competing speciﬁcation reveals a
diﬀerent pattern. For 1.5B, the posthoc diﬀerence
in distractor-only success between competing and ir-
relevant contexts is 8.97 percentage points, interval
[3.21, 16.67], across 39 complete matched tasks; Holm
p = 0 .03125. Versus isolation, the estimate is 9.62
points, interval [3.21, 17.95], in the same four-test
family. The 0.5B competing-minus-irrelevant esti-
mate is 3.12 points, interval [-3.12, 11.25], p = 1. The
stronger 1.5B error-type association therefore does
not imply a similarly large change in its overall target
pass rate.

5.3 Initial context load and static
interruptions
Initial context load shows a near-minus-irrelevant
estimate of -27.50 points for 0.5B, interval [-45.00,
-10.00], Holm p = 0 .033691, and -2.50 for 1.5B, inter-
val [-17.50, 12.50], p = 1 . These selected populations
and weaker distractors cannot be pooled with the re-
vised semantic experiment. Diﬀerences in selection
and distractor construction prevent interpreting the
larger initial estimate as a stronger version of the re-
vised experiment.
The six primary interruption comparisons do not es-
tablish a corrected eﬀect. For example, three other-
domain descriptions versus neutral material on 1.5B
give −15 points with interval [−27.5, −2.5], but Holm
p = 0.421875. This illustrates the distinction between
pointwise intervals and family tests. Its constructed
history and coarse domain labels also preclude a gen-
eral claim about real task switching. Isolated strat-
egy diﬀerences are similarly uncertain: P3_1 minus
P1 is -2.25 points on 0.5B, interval [-7.77, 2.86], and
2.64 on 1.5B, interval [-1.22, 6.30]. Together, these
comparisons show why neither additional history nor
a reminder can be assumed to have a consistent eﬀect
across the tested settings.

6 What Is Gained by Keeping
Less Context?
The retention experiment compares ways of present-
ing the same target amid a ﬁxed history. It asks
whether keeping less information, or replaying the
target, changes correctness and the cost of obtain-
ing a correct answer. All ﬁve policies are shown in
Table 1 and Figure 7. Oracle retention’s correctness
diﬀerence from full history is +7.5 points on 0.5B, in-
terval [−5, 22.5], and +2.5 on 1.5B, interval [−10, 15].
All six registered replay/oracle/compaction accuracy
contrasts have Holm p = 1 . Replay is not established
to improve accuracy, and neither is equivalent accu-
racy established by these wide intervals.
Although the accuracy comparisons remain uncer-
tain, the observed cost ratios diﬀer substantially.
Estimated net energy per correct solution is 325.38
mWh for full history and 76.39 for oracle retention on
0.5B; corresponding 1.5B values are 154.16 and 50.20
mWh. These ratios correspond to observed estimated-
cost reductions of 76.5% and 67.4%, computed as
100(1 − Coracle/Cfull). They are descriptive reductions,
without an established accuracy-preservation guar-
antee. Paired oracle-minus-full ratio diﬀerences are
-249.00 mWh, interval [-529.62, -127.49], and -103.96
mWh, interval [-152.30, -77.58]. These intervals re-
sample complete paired task clusters and recompute
both ratios; they are not intervals on the energy of
successful requests alone.
End- and midpoint-baseline subtraction retain the
lower oracle cost estimates in these data. Exclud-
ing drifted observations also retains the direction, on
smaller matched populations. Cap exclusion reduces
the 0.5B paired diﬀerence to approximately −112.65
mWh over 35 tasks. Some short-window-excluded
0.5B ratio draws have zero correct solutions; ﬁnite
intervals are deliberately left unavailable. In 1.5B or-
acle retention, removing short windows leaves only 18
of 40 calls. It preferentially removes cheap requests,
rather than producing a cleaner estimate for the orig-
inal population.

ment worth investigating, but the oracle has an ad-

7 What Does the Preference
Test Measure?
I designed the preference experiment to ask whether
a buried code-only instruction is followed less often
than an immediate instruction, and whether repeat-
ing it helps. The registered score accepts parseable
Python deﬁning the target function only when it is
not enclosed in Markdown fences. It accepts zero
out of 400 full-study responses: every output fails
this particular rule, leaving no variation for either
primary placement/reminder comparison. The in-
struction asks for code only but does not explicitly
prohibit Markdown fences. The zero score therefore
cannot be read as evidence that every preference was
forgotten.
To understand the zero scores, I retained the original
metric and added a separate posthoc format check
recording fences, outside prose, comments, docstrings
and a fence-tolerant syntax predicate. For 1.5B, each
explicit-preference condition has 40/40 fence-tolerant
outputs, whereas no-preference has 21/40. For 0.5B,
explicit conditions range from 36/40 to 39/40, ver-
sus 15/40 without the preference. These descriptive
diﬀerences do not establish long-term retention: im-
mediate, buried and replayed conditions also diﬀer
in rendered wording and position, and the registered
strict contrasts remain unchanged. Buried/replay
comparisons under the fence-tolerant predicate do
not establish a beneﬁt after correction.
Comments and docstrings can be legitimate code or
explanatory content. Mechanical counts do not re-
place human semantic adherence annotations. Joint
correctness-and-adherence outcomes are provided
under each deﬁnition rather than silently selecting
the most favorable score. A zero-width empirical
bootstrap interval at an all-zero/all-one sample is
not certainty about a population rate. This scoring-
deﬁnition problem is itself an honest limitation of the
experiment.

8.1 From Oracle Retention to
Practical Memory Retrieval
MemoryGraph addresses the step before genera-

model. Its implementation is examined at commit

stores editable local plain-text memories; TF-IDF
and pretrained sentence embeddings rank candi-
dates. A NetworkX bipartite graph links memo-
ries to a small ﬁxed entity vocabulary. The graph-
assisted method adds a bounded bonus to lexical
scores for shared entities. This is not full GraphRAG,
automatic memory extraction, or learned feedback
adaptation. Logistic regression and random-forest
pipelines are evaluation baselines, not a demon-

9 Discussion
The experiments trace a progression from prompt de-
sign to context selection. Prompt-Energy asked what
an instruction costs to execute. Here the question is
also which instruction the model follows when sev-
eral are available. The cross-speciﬁcation test makes
this distinction visible: some wrong answers satisfy a
competing requirement rather than simply failing ev-
ery tested contract. Cross-scoring isolation responses
provides a reference for how often that behavior oc-
curs without competing context. The 1.5B complete
cases show an association, while conservative treat-
ment of incomplete generations weakens it. Recency,
repetition, pattern completion and ordinary bound-
ary errors remain possible explanations rather than
mechanisms identiﬁed by these measurements.
Context length alone is consequently an incomplete
description of the problem. Extra relevant material
may help, while competing material can introduce a
diﬀerent answer that still looks reasonable. The task-
bundle results suggest that content can matter even
beside matched ﬁller, although shared tasks limit the
inference. The uncertain interruption and replay com-
parisons also caution against expecting one consistent
eﬀect from a longer history or a reminder.
The retention results connect this behavioral question
to eﬃciency. Shorter oracle contexts have lower ob-
served estimated energy per correct solution, but ac-
curacy preservation has not been established. Shorter
inputs change preﬁll work; wording and position can
change generated output; output length and failed at-
tempts change the cost ratio. The oracle also avoids
the work of discovering what is relevant. A practical
policy must be evaluated with that selection cost in-
cluded, rather than inherit the oracle’s advantage by
assumption.
Retrieval is therefore an intermediate measurement.
A system must select useful material, handle conﬂicts
and produce an answer that applies the current re-
quirement. In the worked example, the target is avail-
able but the code follows the competing boundary. In
MemoryGraph’s capped demonstration, the pytest
preference is available but the response uses unittest.
Neither illustration estimates a general failure rate;
both explain why a good retrieval rank alone cannot
demonstrate successful utilization.
MemoryGraph oﬀers a concrete next setting for inves-
tigating these questions. A future comparison could
measure retrieval relevance, target correctness, prefer-
ence adherence and complete computational overhead
under matched instructions and output budgets. It
should also validate the adherence metric before col-
lecting outcomes: the all-zero registered preference
score here shows how an unsuitable formatting rule
can obscure the intended question. The existing pro-
totype and synthetic ranking benchmark establish a
starting point, not a completed evaluation of selective
memory in use.

larger models, other quantizations, GPUs or cloud

11 Conclusion
I investigated how additional and competing context
relates to code correctness and inference cost in two
small local language models. Testing a failed answer
against a competing speciﬁcation provides informa-
tion that a single pass/fail score misses. The 1.5B
complete cases show more competing-requirement
failures under competing context, although conserva-
tive assumptions about incomplete outputs weaken
that ﬁnding. Oracle retention has lower observed es-
timated energy per correct solution, with accuracy
preservation unresolved and several policy diﬀerences
confounded. The preference experiment adds a mea-
surement lesson: a rule that rejects every response
cannot answer the intended retention question. To-
gether, these ﬁndings motivate evaluating context
selection through both the behavior it produces and
its total computational cost. MemoryGraph provides
an implemented direction for that subsequent investi-
gation.

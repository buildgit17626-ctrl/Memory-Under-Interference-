Memory Under Interference:
Context Utilization, Specification Confusion, and Inference Efficiency
in Small Code Language Models
Abstract
Increasing a language model’s context makes more infor-
mation available, but also adds computational work and
potentially competing instructions. When does that ex-
tra information help a model solve the intended task? I
investigate this question through seven studies contain-
ing 5,528 experimental requests on HumanEval+-based
coding tasks using Qwen2.5-Coder 0.5B and 1.5B mod-
els with mixed-precision four-bit weights, running on a
CPU laptop. The experiments vary task descriptions, com-
peting requirements, interruptions, retained context and
the placement of a code-only preference. In addition to
testing the requested solution, I test whether an incorrect
answer satisfies a competing requirement. This post-hoc
analysis, formulated after data collection, finds 8.97 per-
centage points more such failures under competing than
irrelevant context on 1.5B (95% task-bootstrap interval
[3.21, 16.67]). The 0.5B comparison is inconclusive, and
conservative assumptions about incomplete generations
weaken the 1.5B result. When only known relevant in-
formation was retained, the estimated net CPU-package
energy per correct solution decreased by 76.5% for the
0.5B model and 67.4% for the 1.5B model, compared with
retaining the full history. However, accuracy preservation
remains uncertain, and differences in prompt length, posi-
tion, instructions and generated output may also contribute
to these savings. The results motivate evaluating memory
management through the answers it produces and its total
computational cost, alongside the relevance of the informa-
tion it supplies.
1 Introduction
A coding assistant often receives more than the task it
must solve. Its context may contain earlier requests, ex-
amples, preferences and descriptions of related problems.
Keeping that history can preserve useful information. It
can also leave an old requirement beside a new one, in-
creasing both the amount of text to process and the chance
of producing a plausible answer to the wrong problem.
The central question is whether the model uses the in-
formation needed for the current task, rather than simply
whether that information fits in its context.
This question grew out of Prompt-Energy, my earlier ex-
periment on how prompt design relates to code correct-
ness, token use, latency and estimated inference energy.
Its implementation and report showed me why response
length and the cost of unsuccessful attempts belong in an
evaluation of prompting. The present study asks a broader
question: what happens when context accumulates, and
useful instructions appear alongside irrelevant or compet-
ing information? Prompt-Energy motivated this work, but
its earlier observations are a separate dataset and are not
pooled with the experiments reported here.
Existing research shows that context position matters, that
conversation history can interfere with a new task, and that
preferences become difficult to follow in long interactions
[4, 7, 15]. Other work finds benefits from combining tasks
[11]. More context is therefore neither inherently useful
nor inherently harmful. I examine when its content matters
for two small local code models, using executable tests
to distinguish the requested behavior from a competing
behavior.
Three questions guide the experiments. RQ1: Does addi-
tional task content change correctness compared with sim-
ilarly long neutral text? RQ2: When an answer is wrong,
does it satisfy a competing requirement present in context?
RQ3: How do context-retention policies and preference
instructions relate to correctness and computational cost?
The contribution is a set of paired code-task comparisons
with inspectable prompts and outputs, a cross-specification
diagnosis of failures, and an evaluation of cost per correct
solution that includes failed attempts. The comparisons
reveal a mixed picture: the larger model’s error distribu-
tion changes in the complete cases, reduced context has
lower observed estimated cost, and several accuracy and
reminder comparisons remain uncertain. The post-hoc
analysis and its sensitivity to incomplete generations are
reported alongside the positive findings.
These results lead naturally to selective context manage-
ment. To explore that practical direction, I developed
MemoryGraph, a local prototype for storing, organizing
and retrieving historical information before it enters a
prompt. It combines lexical and embedding retrieval with
a small entity graph and lets the user inspect and approve
selected memories. Its separate synthetic evaluation mea-
sures ranking; downstream correctness and energy benefits
remain untested. I describe the existing prototype after the
context experiments, connecting the measurement ques-
tions to a system that could be evaluated in subsequent
work.

Preferences and memory systems. PrefEval examines

already assembled prompts, so they examine code gener-

memory store.
Code correctness and computational cost. HumanEval
evaluates generated functions through execution [ 1].
EvalPlus adds tests and demonstrates that limited test
coverage can change both measured correctness and
model rankings [6]. I use its expanded benchmark with
the Qwen2.5-Coder family [ 5]. Inference-energy research
links cost to output length, prompts and runtime choices
[10]; a directly related 2026 study examines prompting,
code accuracy and environmental cost across small and
open models [9]. The present measurements concern CPU-
package energy on one laptop, without transferring carbon
or hardware conclusions from those studies.
Retrieving useful history. GraphRAG uses graph in-
dexes and community summaries for questions about a
corpus [3]; Mem0 evaluates a more integrated persistent-
memory system [2]. MemoryGraph is a smaller compan-
ion prototype with local memory editing, ranking and a ba-
sic entity graph. Its role here is to identify a practical next
evaluation, not to claim the architecture or demonstrated
benefits of these systems. The accompanying related-work
table records the overlap and distinctions in this focused
literature review; it is not a systematic search or a claim of
priority.
3 Evaluation Framework
The experiments separate four ideas. Availability means
that the needed specification is present in the prompt. Re-
trieval is the preceding act of selecting stored material,
illustrated separately by MemoryGraph. Utilization is
measured through what the generated function does on
its tests. Efficiency concerns tokens, latency and estimated
CPU-package energy. These observable outcomes do not
measure internal memory capacity or cognitive states.
An incorrect solution can still be informative. Suppose the
target requires a strict comparison, while a background
task includes equality. A solution using the inclusive
rule is wrong for the target but may be right for that back-
ground task. I test each complete response against target
suite 𝑇 and alternative suite 𝐴, distinguishing target-only
pass, alternative-only pass, both pass and both fail. An
alternative-consistent failure is thus an incorrect target
solution that satisfies the competing computational re-
quirement. “Specification confusion” names this observed
pattern, not a diagnosis of the model’s internal reasoning.
Finite tests, extraction errors and ordinary algorithmic mis-
takes remain relevant to interpreting every cell.

4 Experimental Methodology
4.1 Models, tasks and execution
The models are qwen2.5-coder:0.5b and
qwen2.5-coder:1.5b, both using Q4_K_M quantiza-
tion, a mixed-precision format that stores most model
weights at four bits. Exact model digests are recorded in
frozen jobs. Inference used Ollama on Windows 11, an In-
tel i5-13420H CPU and approximately 16 GB RAM, with
eight inference threads and num_gpu=0. Generation was
sequential, with randomized interleaved condition sched-
ules. Recorded block-start checks observed AC power
and Best performance; these snapshots do not prove an
invariant host state throughout every request. Decoding
settings, templates, native token counts, raw outputs and
retry records are retained.
An experimental request means one scheduled task/con-
dition/model/sample call; a retry does not create another
independent observation. The seven studies contain the
following requests, including documented failures: iso-
lated strategy comparison, 1,968; initial context load plus
its isolation controls, 280; task bundles, 1,040; retention,
400; revised semantic interference plus isolation, 1,040;
static interruptions plus isolation, 400; and preference
placement, 400. These sum directly to 5,528. Pilots are

4.2 What the context comparisons test
To separate reading extra tasks from solving them, pas-
sive load adds other task descriptions, while active load
requests their functions as well. 𝐾 ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6} ; neu-
tral filler matches passive input length. Target placement
and companions are fixed. Active requests do more out-
put work, so focal-target, per-function and whole-bundle
endpoints are kept distinct. Reused component-isolation
outputs are lookup references, not newly measured bundle
execution.
To test whether a competing requirement changes the an-
swer, the revised interference experiment crosses three
filler types, filler-token targets 1,000/2,000 and target be-
ginning/end, with matched isolation controls. Competing
descriptions change a computational requirement, such as
strict versus non-strict comparison or inclusive versus ex-
clusive boundaries. Alternative references and distinguish-
ing inputs were validated. The model sees descriptions,
not the alternative implementation or hidden tests. Relat-
ed/irrelevant labels are authored coarse categories; they
are not calibrated semantic similarity. Filler budgets do
not guarantee identical complete input lengths across ev-
ery wrapper, an important limit on attributing differences
solely to content.
To examine the cost of keeping history, I compare full his-
tory, target/query only, verbatim target replay, lossless
target-format compaction, and oracle retention. Oracle re-
tention means selecting context using known relevance
labels: the target specification and preference are kept
without having to discover which history is relevant. Eight
fixed distractor descriptions form full history. No-history
omits the explicit code-only preference, while the oracle
retains target, preference and final query. Compaction
removes redundant target formatting and drops other his-
tory; it is not semantic summarization of a conversation.
These policies jointly change length, position, repetition
and sometimes instruction content.
To examine returning to a task after intervening descrip-
tions, the static interruption conditions contain neutral
material, one similar competing description, one other-
domain description, or three other-domain descriptions.
Loaded complete input counts match, with the same tar-
get and final instruction. Descriptions and acknowledge-
ments are literal transcripts, not tasks executed earlier by
the model. Similarity, count and diversity are not fully
crossed. Finally, to test whether a reminder helps with an
output preference, preference placement compares absent,
immediate, buried, replayed and corrected code-only in-
structions while preserving the target/query and distractor
history.
4.3 Statistics, exclusions and energy
Intervals use 2,000 paired task-bootstrap draws with seed
1732. Binary pairs use exact discordant-pair McNemar
tests; task means over repeated cells use exact paired sign
swaps. Holm multiple-comparison correction adjusts test
𝑝-values to control the family-wise probability of one or
more false rejections. It is applied within specified fami-
lies: two isolated strategy tests, two initial-load primary
tests, two revised-interference primary tests, six retention
tests and six interruption tests. The new cross-pass audit
uses four post-hoc tests. These corrections do not control
every analysis ever examined in the project. Intervals are
pointwise, not simultaneous family intervals. Null tests do
not establish equivalent accuracy.
I preserve failed requests and capped generations. Caps
are nonpasses for bounded correctness endpoints but un-
known for the uncensored cross-pass taxonomy. API,
input or evaluator-infrastructure failures are missing

5 Contextual Interference
5.1 Does task content matter beyond length?
The task-bundle experiment asks whether similarly long
contexts behave differently when the extra text describes
other problems. Figure 2 compares correctness of the des-
ignated target function under passive, active and neutral
conditions. Passive-minus-filler estimates tend to be neg-
ative across 𝐾 in this roster, but many adjusted compar-
isons are inconclusive. The 0.5B 𝐾 = 6 estimate is −25
percentage points, conditional interval [−37.5, −12.5],
with Holm 𝑝 = 0.015625 in its eight-comparison post-hoc
family. This is evidence about the fixed task combinations
tested here, rather than a general penalty for multitasking.
Tasks recur in several bundles, and all bundles are con-
nected through shared members. The bootstrap therefore
describes variation across target tasks while keeping their
companions fixed; it cannot supply uncertainty for inde-
pendently resampled bundles. Changing 𝐾 also changes
which companion functions appear. Active per-function
fractions and whole-bundle successes are reported against
the same components’ isolation outcomes in the archive,
rather than equating a passive focal pass with a complete
active bundle. The additional output budget and repeated
component references limit cost comparisons. The shared
companions and additional output work are important
alternative explanations when comparing bundle perfor-
mance and cost.
5.2 Does a wrong answer follow the
competing requirement?
The first comparison asks whether competing descriptions
reduce target correctness relative to irrelevant descriptions.
The registered difference is -11.25 percentage points for
0.5B, interval [-20.62, -2.50], Holm 𝑝 = 0.056396 , and
-2.50 for 1.5B, interval [-10.00, 4.39], Holm 𝑝 = 0.63525 .
Both comparisons have 40 paired tasks averaged over four
length/position cells. A pointwise interval excluding zero
can coexist with an adjusted test that does not reject; both
are reported. Figure 3 shows the cell-level outcomes with-
out treating them as independent task replications.
Overall accuracy does not describe which requirement a
wrong answer follows. Testing the same saved programs
against the competing specification reveals a different pat-
tern. For 1.5B, the post-hoc difference in distractor-only
success between competing and irrelevant contexts is 8.97
percentage points, interval [3.21, 16.67], across 39 com-
plete matched tasks; Holm 𝑝 = 0.03125 . V ersus isola-
tion, the estimate is 9.62 points, interval [3.21, 17.95], in
the same four-test family. The 0.5B competing-minus-
irrelevant estimate is 3.12 points, interval [-3.12, 11.25],
𝑝 = 1 . The stronger 1.5B error-type association therefore
does not imply a similarly large change in its overall target
pass rate.
Figure 5 includes every scheduled response, whereas
complete-case inference requires all paired cells. No re-
sponse passes both suites in this dataset. Figure 4 makes
one alternative-only outcome concrete. The target spec-
ification is present and marked as the only function to
implement, yet the saved body uses the competing inclu-
sive boundary. A second audited 1.5B response reverses
parenthesis-group order as required by its alternative.
These selected examples illustrate the test categories; they
do not estimate their prevalence or reveal why the model
produced those programs.
Incomplete or invalid responses leave some classifica-
tions unknown. As a conservative check, I assign those
unknown outcomes in whichever way weakens the ob-
served association: none counts as an alternative-only
success under competing context, and every unknown
control does. This yields a 1.5B difference of 6.88 points
over all 40 tasks, scenario interval [−0.02, 15.00], adjusted
𝑝 = 0.53125 . The favorable assignment gives 10.62 points,
interval [3.75, 18.75]. These adverse and favorable assign-
ments describe sensitivity to missing outcomes. They are
hypothetical classifications, not repaired responses or a
formal confidence set for missingness. The complete-case
result is therefore suggestive but not robust to all cap as-
signments (Figure 6).

6 What Is Gained by Keeping Less
Context?
The retention experiment compares ways of presenting
the same target amid a fixed history. It asks whether keep-
ing less information, or replaying the target, changes cor-
rectness and the cost of obtaining a correct answer. All
five policies are shown in Table 1 and Figure 7. Oracle re-
tention’s correctness difference from full history is +7.5
points on 0.5B, interval [−5, 22.5], and +2.5 on 1.5B,
interval [−10, 15]. All six registered replay/oracle/com-
paction accuracy contrasts have Holm 𝑝 = 1 . Replay is not
established to improve accuracy, and neither is equivalent
accuracy established by these wide intervals.
Although the accuracy comparisons remain uncertain, the
observed cost ratios differ substantially. Estimated net
energy per correct solution is 325.38 mWh for full his-
tory and 76.39 for oracle retention on 0.5B; corresponding
1.5B values are 154.16 and 50.20 mWh. These ratios cor-
respond to observed estimated-cost reductions of 76.5%
and 67.4%, computed as 100(1 − 𝐶 oracle/𝐶full). They are
descriptive reductions, without an established accuracy-
preservation guarantee. Paired oracle-minus-full ratio dif-
ferences are -249.00 mWh, interval [-529.62, -127.49],
and -103.96 mWh, interval [-152.30, -77.58]. These inter-
vals resample complete paired task clusters and recompute
both ratios; they are not intervals on the energy of success-
ful requests alone.
End- and midpoint-baseline subtraction retain the lower
oracle cost estimates in these data. Excluding drifted ob-
servations also retains the direction, on smaller matched
populations. Cap exclusion reduces the 0.5B paired differ-
ence to approximately −112.65 mWh over 35 tasks. Some
short-window-excluded 0.5B ratio draws have zero correct
solutions; finite intervals are deliberately left unavailable.
In 1.5B oracle retention, removing short windows leaves
only 18 of 40 calls. It preferentially removes cheap re-
quests, rather than producing a cleaner estimate for the
original population.

ment worth investigating, but the oracle has an advantage
a practical system must earn: it is told what matters. Full

7 What Does the Preference Test
Measure?
I designed the preference experiment to ask whether a
buried code-only instruction is followed less often than
an immediate instruction, and whether repeating it helps.
The registered score accepts parseable Python defining the
target function only when it is not enclosed in Markdown
fences. It accepts zero out of 400 full-study responses: ev-
ery output fails this particular rule, leaving no variation
for either primary placement/reminder comparison. The
instruction asks for code only but does not explicitly pro-
hibit Markdown fences. The zero score therefore cannot
be read as evidence that every preference was forgotten.
To understand the zero scores, I retained the original met-
ric and added a separate post-hoc format check record-
ing fences, outside prose, comments, docstrings and a
fence-tolerant syntax predicate. For 1.5B, each explicit-
preference condition has 40/40 fence-tolerant outputs,
whereas no-preference has 21/40. For 0.5B, explicit condi-
tions range from 36/40 to 39/40, versus 15/40 without the
preference. These descriptive differences do not establish
long-term retention: immediate, buried and replayed con-
ditions also differ in rendered wording and position, and
the registered strict contrasts remain unchanged. Buried/re-
play comparisons under the fence-tolerant predicate do not
establish a benefit after correction.
Comments and docstrings can be legitimate code or ex-
planatory content. Mechanical counts do not replace hu-
man semantic adherence annotations. Joint correctness-
and-adherence outcomes are provided under each defini-
tion rather than silently selecting the most favorable score.
A zero-width empirical bootstrap interval at an all-zero/all-
one sample is not certainty about a population rate. This
scoring-definition problem is itself an honest limitation of
the experiment.

8.1 From Oracle Retention to Practical
Memory Retrieval
MemoryGraph addresses the step before genera-
tion: choosing useful historical information with-
out automatically passing the entire history to the
model. Its implementation is examined at commit
c569143ec6be1c767a2c56c59c936f85bd1fc1cb. It
stores editable local plain-text memories; term-frequency–
inverse-document-frequency (TF-IDF) lexical scores and
pretrained sentence embeddings rank candidates. A Net-
workX bipartite graph links memories to a small fixed
entity vocabulary. The graph-assisted method adds a
bounded bonus to lexical scores for shared entities. This
is not full GraphRAG, automatic memory extraction, or
learned feedback adaptation. Logistic regression and
random-forest pipelines are evaluation baselines, not a
demonstrated learned online memory controller.
User control matters because a highly ranked memory may
still be obsolete, conflicting or unwanted in the current
context. The interface exposes selected text and requires
explicit approval before a local Ollama response compari-
son. Limited warnings detect explicit pytest versus unittest
and VS Code versus PyCharm preference conflicts; they
do not resolve arbitrary contradictions. No usability, trust,
personalization or energy benefit was measured. Figure 9
describes the existing components only.
My saved synthetic evaluation contains 600 labelled
query–memory pairs, 24 fictional profiles and 120 queries
with five candidate memories each. Exactly one candi-
date is labelled relevant. Recomputed embedding Mean
Reciprocal Rank (MRR), the mean of one divided by the
relevant memory’s rank for each query, is 0.7364, ver-
sus 0.5163 for TF-IDF, using the saved scores and stable
tie ordering. The embedding snapshot and data hashes
are retained. The offline lexical benchmark fits its vo-
cabulary across the saved corpus; the interactive lexical
retriever instead fits the query and candidate memories.
MRR summarizes how early the relevant memory appears
in a ranked list; it does not score the subsequent answer.
Because the two lexical pipelines fit different vocabularies,
the benchmark also does not directly measure interface
ranking. These are template-based synthetic rankings, not
held-out code-generation outcomes or estimates of real-
user behavior. Grouped classifier evaluation splits by fic-
tional profile, but repeated templates can still supply short-
cuts; the random forest’s recorded perfect synthetic result
should not be read as general memory-understanding abil-

ble: some wrong answers satisfy a competing requirement

The 1.5B complete cases show an association, while con-

it. Recency, repetition, pattern completion and ordinary
boundary errors remain possible explanations rather than
mechanisms identified by these measurements.
Context length alone is consequently an incomplete de-
scription of the problem. Extra relevant material may help,
while competing material can introduce a different answer
that still looks reasonable. The task-bundle results sug-
gest that content can matter even beside matched filler,
although shared tasks limit the inference. The uncertain
interruption and replay comparisons also caution against
expecting one consistent effect from a longer history or a
reminder.
The retention results connect this behavioral question to
efficiency. Shorter oracle contexts have lower observed
estimated energy per correct solution, but accuracy preser-
vation has not been established. Shorter inputs change pre-
fill work, the computation used to process the input before
generating tokens; wording and position can change gener-
ated output; output length and failed attempts change the
cost ratio. The oracle also avoids the work of discovering
what is relevant. A practical policy must be evaluated with
that selection cost included, rather than inherit the oracle’s
advantage by assumption.
Retrieval is therefore an intermediate measurement. A sys-
tem must select useful material, handle conflicts and pro-
duce an answer that applies the current requirement. In the
worked example, the target is available but the code fol-
lows the competing boundary. In MemoryGraph’s capped
demonstration, the pytest preference is available but the
response uses unittest. Neither illustration estimates a gen-
eral failure rate; both explain why a good retrieval rank
alone cannot demonstrate successful utilization.
MemoryGraph offers a concrete next setting for investigat-
ing these questions. A future comparison could measure
retrieval relevance, target correctness, preference adher-
ence and complete computational overhead under matched
instructions and output budgets. It should also validate the
adherence metric before collecting outcomes: the all-zero
registered preference score here shows how an unsuitable
formatting rule can obscure the intended question. The ex-
isting prototype and synthetic ranking benchmark establish
a starting point, not a completed evaluation of selective
memory in use.
10 Limitations
These results concern two small quantized models, one
code benchmark and one CPU laptop. Training-set expo-
sure is unknown; benchmark familiarity is neither ruled
out nor inferred from a pass. Fixed task rosters, noisy el-
igibility selection, one structural generation per cell and
only three isolated stochastic samples limit generalization.
Shared functions connect M1 bundles and prevent robust
component-level intervals. The smaller-model effects need
not predict larger models, other quantizations, GPUs or
cloud deployments.
The study developed through revisions and is partly ex-
ploratory. Registrations and dated amendments are pre-
served, but the final synthesis and cross-specification audit
are post-hoc. Local Git hashes support provenance, not
independent public timestamping. Family correction does
not erase prior exploration. The separate synthetic pro-
totype benchmark is small and templated, with no user
study.
Scoring depends on finite test suites, extraction and a Win-
dows restricted subprocess adapter. These controls are
documented rather than described as a complete adversar-
ial sandbox. Alternative construction and similarity labels
are AI-assisted and authored; representative witnesses are
not exhaustive semantic validation. Adverse censoring
weakens the principal cross-pass result. No recognition,
internal state or intentional giving-up measurement exists.
CPU-package estimates are not calibrated process-specific
energy. Unrelated work, shared baselines, clock/sampling
gaps, thermal effects and approximate post-load bound-
aries remain potential errors. Bootstrap intervals reflect
task variation, not measurement calibration or independent
block errors. Short requests are particularly difficult at 500
ms resolution. The incomplete registered cache experi-
ment prevents a completed primary energy claim.
11 Conclusion
I investigated how additional and competing context re-
lates to code correctness and inference cost in two small
9
Memory Under Interference Preprint – V ersion 1.1
local language models. Testing a failed answer against a
competing specification provides information that a sin-
gle pass/fail score misses. The 1.5B complete cases show
more competing-requirement failures under competing
context, although conservative assumptions about incom-
plete outputs weaken that finding. Oracle retention has
lower observed estimated energy per correct solution, with
accuracy preservation unresolved and several policy differ-
ences confounded. The preference experiment adds a mea-
surement lesson: a rule that rejects every response cannot
answer the intended retention question. Together, these
findings motivate evaluating context selection through
both the behavior it produces and its total computational
cost. MemoryGraph provides an implemented direction
for that subsequent investigation.

Memory Under Interference:
Context Utilization, Speciﬁcation Confusion, and Inference Eﬃciency
in Small Code Language Models
Dhruv Brahmbhatt
IIT Madras student; independent researcher ∗
24 November 2026
Preprint – Version 1.0 – Not peer reviewed
Abstract
Relevant information can be present in a language
model’s context while its generated code follows a com-
peting requirement. We investigate this gap in two
small quantized Qwen2.5-Coder models on one CPU
laptop. Seven completed studies contain 5,528 main-
study logical requests, including documented failures;
pilots and an incomplete cache study remain sepa-
rate. Controlled contexts vary task load, competing
speciﬁcations, static interruptions, retention policies
and a code-only preference. Cross-speciﬁcation eval-
uation tests saved programs against both target and
alternative requirements. In a posthoc paired analy-
sis, the 1.5B model has 8.97 percentage points more
alternative-consistent failures under competing than
irrelevant context (95% task-bootstrap interval [3.21,
16.67]; 39 matched tasks). The 0.5B result is inconclu-
sive, and adverse coding of censored outcomes weakens
the 1.5B ﬁnding. Relative to full history, oracle reten-
tion has observed estimated net CPU-package energy-
per-correct-solution reductions of 76.5% and 67.4% for
0.5B and 1.5B. Accuracy preservation is uncertain;
length, position, instructions and output work diﬀer,
so these reductions cannot be attributed exclusively
to retention. A registered preference metric reaches a
ﬂoor that a separate format audit helps explain. Mem-
oryGraph is a companion retrieval prototype with a
separate synthetic evaluation, not an evaluated inter-
vention in these studies. Together, the results moti-
vate testing what retrieved context makes a system do,
alongside what it retrieves and what inference costs.
1 Introduction
Memory-management systems decide which past in-
structions, preferences and task descriptions to put in a
model’s next prompt. More context can preserve useful
information, but it can also keep obsolete or compet-
ing requirements in view. For a coding assistant, the
consequence may be a program that is sensible for the
wrong speciﬁcation. A useful evaluation must therefore
connect context selection to executable behavior and
computational cost, rather than judge success from the

presence of relevant text alone.
Prior work already shows position-sensitive context
use, conversational task interference, and diﬃculty fol-
lowing user preferences [ 4, 7, 15]. Multi-task inference
can also help some tasks [ 11]; degradation is not a uni-
versal consequence of bundling. We examine these es-
tablished concerns in two small local code models, us-
ing execution-based outcomes and CPU-package mea-
surements on consumer hardware.
Three research questions organize the report. RQ1:
How does task-bearing context compare with length-
matched neutral material? RQ2: Are target failures
more often consistent with a competing speciﬁcation
when that speciﬁcation appears in context? RQ3:
How do retention and preference policies relate to cor-
rectness and inference cost? These are questions about
observable programs, not internal memory capacity or
cognitive states.
Contributions. We provide (1) a controlled
contextual-interference evaluation with paired
code tasks, matched controls and distinct task-
load/interruption constructions; (2) a cross-
speciﬁcation failure diagnosis that separates target-
only, alternative-only, both-pass and neither-pass
outcomes, with isolation cross-scoring and posthoc
censoring analyses; and (3) an evaluation of context-
retention correctness and computational-eﬃciency
trade-oﬀs that includes failed-attempt cost and uncer-
tainty in cost per correct solution. Sanitized journals,
exact prompts and portable analysis accompany the
report.
The ﬁndings are mixed. Competing context changes
the larger model’s observed error distribution in
the complete cases, but censoring weakens the in-
ference. Oracle retention has lower observed esti-
mated costs, without established accuracy preserva-
tion. A preference-scoring ﬂoor and several null com-
parisons show where the evidence is less informative.
The seven-study collection grew from Prompt-Energy
through dated design revisions; registered comparisons
and posthoc analyses remain distinguished throughout.
The separate MemoryGraph prototype connects these
questions to practical retrieval without supplying an
integrated performance claim.

2 Related Work
Context and instruction interference. Lost in
the Middle studies relevant-information position in
question answering and retrieval [ 7]. Gupta et al. ex-
amine task switches in conversation history [ 4]; our
static interruption study is closely related, but does not
contain actual preceding model task execution. Son et
al. ﬁnd that multi-task inference can improve perfor-
mance and speed on their benchmark [ 11]. Our much
smaller code models and diﬀerent tasks do not contra-
dict that result merely because some bundled condi-
tions perform worse. Instruction Hierarchy addresses
conﬂicting privilege levels and training for robust prior-
itization [12]. We neither train a hierarchy nor evaluate
adversarial security robustness.
Memory and preferences. PrefEval examines ex-
plicit and implicit preference following in longer con-
versations [ 15]; our code-only preference and mechan-
ical metric are a much narrower instrument. Long-
MemEval separates indexing, retrieval and reading in
conversational memory systems [ 13]. Recent EvalMem
explicitly diagnoses encoding, retrieval and genera-
tion defects [ 8], while LongMemEval-V2 examines
experience-oriented agent memory [ 14]. Thus, sepa-
rating availability from retrieval and generation is not
itself a novelty claim. Our main studies do not im-
plement a memory store or diagnose encoding defects;
they test already-rendered code-task contexts.
Code evaluation and eﬃciency . HumanEval in-
troduced execution-based code-generation evaluation
[1]; EvalPlus expands its tests and demonstrates the
consequences of insuﬃcient oracles [ 6]. Qwen2.5-Coder
supplies the model family [ 5]. Prior inference-energy
benchmarking relates cost to outputs, prompts and
runtime settings [ 10]. A directly overlapping 2026
study evaluates prompting, code accuracy and environ-
mental cost across small/open models [ 9]. We do not
claim the ﬁrst prompt-energy study, nor transfer its
hardware or carbon results to this laptop.
Practical retrieval. GraphRAG builds graph in-
dexes and community summaries for corpus-level ques-
tions [ 3]; Mem0 studies a more integrated persistent-
memory system [2]. Our companion MemoryGraph im-
plements far less. Its small entity graph and synthetic
retrieval results motivate a future bridge, not a claim of
equivalent architecture or evaluated user beneﬁt. The
release’s related-work table records each publication’s
design, overlap and the limited distinction supported
here. This is a focused review, not an exhaustive search
or proof of priority.
3 Evaluation Framework
We distinguish four operations. Availability means
that the intended speciﬁcation is literally present in
the rendered prompt. Retrieval means selecting stored
material for a prompt; it is only demonstrated in the
separate companion prototype. Utilization is opera-
tionalized by the generated function’s observed test
outcomes. Eﬃciency describes measured tokens, la-
tency and qualiﬁed CPU-package estimates, including
failed attempts. None is a direct measurement of inter-
nal memory capacity.
For a target program test suite T and a correspond-
ing computational alternative A, an uncensored re-
sponse belongs to one of four cells: target-only pass,
alternative-only pass, both pass, or both fail. We call
the second cell alternative-consistent or distractor-only
success. “Speciﬁcation confusion” in the title refers
only to this operational error pattern. Passing a ﬁnite
suite does not prove general program correctness, and
a diﬀerent failure may reﬂect syntax, extraction, policy
restrictions or ordinary algorithmic error.

4 Experimental Methodology
4.1 Data, runtime and actual study
scope
The models are qwen2.5-coder:0.5b and
qwen2.5-coder:1.5b, both Q4_K_M quantiza-
tions, with exact model digests recorded in frozen
jobs. Inference used Ollama on Windows 11, an Intel
i5-13420H CPU and approximately 16 GB RAM, with
eight inference threads and num_gpu=0. Generation
was sequential, with randomized interleaved condition
schedules. Recorded block-start checks observed AC
power and Best performance; these snapshots do not
prove an invariant host state throughout every request.
Decoding settings, templates, native token counts,
raw outputs and retry records are retained.
The main request counts are: isolated strategy com-
parison, 1,968; initial context load plus its isolation
controls, 280; task bundles, 1,040; retention, 400; re-
vised semantic interference plus isolation, 1,040; static
interruptions plus isolation, 400; and preference place-
ment, 400. These sum directly to 5,528. Pilots are
separate observations. The revised cache study saved
76 of 400 planned full requests and is a partial appendix

4.2 Controlled context constructions
Passive task load adds other task descriptions; active
load requests their functions as well. K ∈ { 2, 3, 4, 6};
neutral ﬁller matches passive input length. Target
placement and companions are ﬁxed. Active requests
do more output work, so focal-target, per-function
and whole-bundle endpoints are kept distinct. Reused
component-isolation outputs are lookup references, not
newly measured bundle execution.
Revised semantic interference crosses three ﬁller
types, ﬁller-token targets 1,000/2,000 and target be-
ginning/end, with matched isolation controls. Com-
peting descriptions change a computational require-
ment, such as strict versus non-strict comparison or
inclusive versus exclusive boundaries. Alternative ref-
erences and distinguishing inputs were validated. The
model sees descriptions, not the alternative implemen-
tation or hidden tests. Related/irrelevant labels are
authored coarse categories; they are not calibrated se-
mantic similarity. Filler budgets do not guarantee iden-
tical complete input lengths across every wrapper, an
important limit on attributing diﬀerences solely to con-
tent.
Retention compares full history, target/query only,
verbatim target replay, lossless target-format com-
paction, and oracle retention. Eight ﬁxed distractor
descriptions form full history. No-history omits the ex-
plicit code-only preference, while the oracle retains tar-
get, preference and ﬁnal query. Compaction removes
redundant target formatting and drops other history;
it is not semantic summarization of a conversation.
These policies jointly change length, position, repeti-
tion and sometimes instruction content.
Static interruption conditions contain neutral ma-
terial, one similar competing description, one other-
domain description, or three other-domain descrip-
tions. Loaded complete input counts match, with the
same target and ﬁnal instruction. Descriptions and
acknowledgements are literal transcripts, not tasks ex-
ecuted earlier by the model. Similarity, count and
diversity are not fully crossed. Preference placement
compares absent, immediate, buried, replayed and cor-
rected code-only instructions while preserving the tar-
get/query and distractor history.
4.3 Statistics, exclusions and energy
Intervals use 2,000 paired task-bootstrap draws with
seed 1732. Binary pairs use exact discordant-pair Mc-
Nemar tests; task means over repeated cells use exact
paired sign swaps. Holm correction is within speciﬁed
families: two isolated strategy tests, two initial-load
primary tests, two revised-interference primary tests,
six retention tests and six interruption tests. The new
cross-pass audit uses four posthoc tests. These correc-
tions do not control every analysis ever examined in
the project. Intervals are pointwise, not simultaneous
family intervals. Null tests do not establish equivalent
accuracy.
The audit preserves failed requests and capped gen-
erations. Caps are nonpasses for bounded correctness
endpoints but unknown for the uncensored cross-pass
taxonomy. API, input or evaluator-infrastructure fail-
ures are missing for valid-input accuracy and sepa-
rately reduce operational yield. We report cap ex-

5 Contextual Interference
5.1 Context content and additional out-
put work
Figure 2 compares focal-target outcomes under passive,
active and neutral conditions. Passive-minus-ﬁller es-
timates tend to be negative across K in this roster,
but many adjusted comparisons are inconclusive. The
0.5B K = 6 estimate is −25 percentage points, condi-
tional interval [−37.5, −12.5], with Holm p = 0.015625
in its eight-comparison posthoc family. This single con-
ditional contrast does not establish a universal multi-
tasking penalty.
Every bundle-membership graph forms one con-
nected component. Anchor-task bootstrap intervals
condition on the ﬁxed companions and cannot esti-
mate dependence-robust variability from other bun-
dles. Changing K also changes which companion func-
tions appear. Active per-function fractions and whole-
bundle successes are reported against the same compo-
nents’ isolation outcomes in the archive, rather than
equating a passive focal pass with a complete active
bundle. The additional output budget and repeated
component references limit cost comparisons. This de-
pendence limitation is substantive, not a small-sample
footnote to a population-level claim.
5.2 Competing speciﬁcations and
alternative-consistent failures
The revised primary competing-minus-irrelevant
target-correctness diﬀerence is -11.25 percentage
points for 0.5B, interval [-20.62, -2.50], Holm
p = 0 .056396, and -2.50 for 1.5B, interval [-10.00,
4.39], Holm p = 0 .63525. Both comparisons have 40
paired tasks averaged over four length/position cells.
A pointwise interval excluding zero can coexist with
an adjusted test that does not reject; both are re-
ported. Figure 3 shows the cell-level outcomes without
treating them as independent task replications.
Cross-scoring reveals a diﬀerent pattern. For 1.5B,
the posthoc diﬀerence in distractor-only success be-
tween competing and irrelevant contexts is 8.97 per-
centage points, interval [3.21, 16.67], across 39 com-
plete matched tasks; Holm p = 0 .03125. Versus isola-
tion, the estimate is 9.62 points, interval [3.21, 17.95],
in the same four-test family. The 0.5B competing-
minus-irrelevant estimate is 3.12 points, interval [-3.12,
11.25], p = 1. The stronger 1.5B error-type association
therefore does not imply a similarly large change in its
overall target pass rate.
Figure 5 includes every scheduled response, whereas
complete-case inference requires all paired cells. No
response passes both suites in this dataset. Figure 4
makes one alternative-only outcome concrete. The tar-
get speciﬁcation is present and marked as the only func-
tion to implement, yet the saved body uses the com-
peting inclusive boundary. A second audited 1.5B re-
sponse reverses parenthesis-group order as required by
its alternative. These selected examples illustrate the
test categories; they do not estimate their prevalence
or reveal why the model produced those programs.
Censoring limits this ﬁnding. Assigning every un-
known competing response against the association and
every unknown control in its favor yields a 1.5B diﬀer-
ence of 6.88 points over all 40 tasks, scenario interval
[−0.02, 15.00], adjusted p = 0 .53125. The favorable
assignment gives 10.62 points, interval [3.75, 18.75].
These are adverse/favorable coding scenarios, not ob-
served repaired outcomes or a formal conﬁdence set
for missingness. The complete-case result is therefore
suggestive but not robust to all cap assignments (Fig-
ure 6).
5.3 Initial context load and static inter-
ruptions
Initial context load shows a near-minus-irrelevant esti-
mate of -27.50 points for 0.5B, interval [-45.00, -10.00],
Holm p = 0.033691, and -2.50 for 1.5B, interval [-17.50,
12.50], p = 1 . These selected populations and weaker
distractors cannot be pooled with the revised semantic
experiment. Nor should the numerically larger initial
eﬀect be taken as evidence of a stronger internal capac-
ity limit.
The six primary interruption comparisons do not es-
tablish a corrected eﬀect. For example, three other-
domain descriptions versus neutral material on 1.5B
give −15 points with interval [−27.5, −2.5], but Holm
p = 0 .421875. This illustrates the distinction between
pointwise intervals and family tests. Its constructed
history and coarse domain labels also preclude a gen-
eral claim about real task switching. Isolated strategy
diﬀerences are similarly uncertain: P3_1 minus P1 is
-2.25 points on 0.5B, interval [-7.77, 2.86], and 2.64 on
1.5B, interval [-1.22, 6.30]. These negative or inconclu-
sive results constrain the narrative.

6 Retention Policies and Com-
putational Eﬃciency
All ﬁve policies are shown in Table 1 and Figure 7. Or-
acle retention’s correctness diﬀerence from full history
is +7.5 points on 0.5B, interval [−5, 22.5], and +2.5 on
1.5B, interval [−10, 15]. All six registered replay/ora-
cle/compaction accuracy contrasts have Holm p = 1 .
Replay is not established to improve accuracy, and nei-
ther is equivalent accuracy established by these wide
intervals.
The measured cost contrast is much larger than the
uncertain accuracy contrast. Estimated net energy
per correct solution is 325.38 mWh for full history
and 76.39 for oracle retention on 0.5B; corresponding
1.5B values are 154.16 and 50.20 mWh. These ratios
correspond to observed estimated-cost reductions of
76.5% and 67.4%, computed as 100(1 − Coracle/Cfull).
They are descriptive reductions, without an established
accuracy-preservation guarantee. Paired oracle-minus-
full ratio diﬀerences are -249.00 mWh, interval [-529.62,
-127.49], and -103.96 mWh, interval [-152.30, -77.58].
These intervals resample complete paired task clusters
and recompute both ratios; they are not intervals on
the energy of successful requests alone.
End- and midpoint-baseline subtraction retain the
lower oracle cost estimates in these data. Exclud-
ing drifted observations also retains the direction, on
smaller matched populations. Cap exclusion reduces
the 0.5B paired diﬀerence to approximately −112.65
mWh over 35 tasks. Some short-window-excluded 0.5B
ratio draws have zero correct solutions; ﬁnite intervals
are deliberately left unavailable. In 1.5B oracle reten-
tion, removing short windows leaves only 18 of 40 calls.
It preferentially removes cheap requests, rather than
producing a cleaner estimate for the original popula-
tion.
Several alternatives remain. Oracle selection knows
what matters, unlike a deployed retriever. Full his-
tory and the reduced policies diﬀer in input length and
recency. No-history omits the code-only instruction
and produces more output tokens; compaction only
changes formatting of retained target text. Generation
budgets, output caps and runtime overhead can also in-
ﬂuence ratios. No automated retrieval/indexing cost is
included. The sample Pareto display (Figure 8) sum-
marizes observed policy trade-oﬀs, not a population-
optimal frontier or proof of accuracy-preserving energy
savings.

7 Preference Utilization
The registered adherence predicate requires unfenced
parseable Python deﬁning the target function. It
scores zero out of 400 full-study responses, so both
primary placement/reminder contrasts are at the ob-
served ﬂoor. The instruction asks for code only but
does not explicitly prohibit Markdown fences. Treat-
ing this ﬂoor as evidence that the model forgot every
preference would therefore overstate what the instru-
ment measures.
A separate posthoc audit preserves the original
scores and records fences, outside prose, comments,
docstrings and a fence-tolerant syntax predicate. For
1.5B, each explicit-preference condition has 40/40
fence-tolerant outputs, whereas no-preference has
21/40. For 0.5B, explicit conditions range from 36/40
to 39/40, versus 15/40 without the preference. These
descriptive diﬀerences do not establish long-term reten-
tion: immediate, buried and replayed conditions also
diﬀer in rendered wording and position, and the reg-
istered strict contrasts remain unchanged. Buried/re-
play comparisons under the fence-tolerant predicate do
not establish a beneﬁt after correction.
Comments and docstrings can be legitimate code
or explanatory content. Mechanical counts do not re-
place human semantic adherence annotations. Joint
correctness-and-adherence outcomes are provided un-
der each deﬁnition rather than silently selecting the
most favorable score. A zero-width empirical boot-
strap interval at an all-zero/all-one sample is not cer-
tainty about a population rate. This scoring-deﬁnition
problem is itself an honest limitation of the experiment.

Retention is also a computational decision.
The observed oracle/full-history cost gap makes selec-
tive context management a useful practical question,
but it does not isolate the beneﬁt of selecting relevant
memories. Shorter input changes preﬁll work; posi-
tion and instructions can change the response; out-
put length and failed attempts change decode cost and
cost per correct solution. Privileged relevance informa-

separate the oracle from a deployed system. Length-
and instruction-matched interventions with end-to-end
overhead would be needed to attribute savings to re-
trieval policy itself. Our uncertain accuracy diﬀerences
do not establish a safe compression or retention rule.
What the null results contribute. Replay is not
consistently beneﬁcial in the registered comparisons,
static interruptions are inconclusive after correction,
and E5’s strict score reaches a ﬂoor partly explained
by its treatment of fences. These outcomes argue for
validating the measurement before choosing a mem-
ory policy. They also narrow the next research step:
test retrieval and generation together under matched
instructions, with a validated adherence rubric, rather
than assume that more retained context or a reminder
must help.
10 Limitations
The results concern two small quantized models, one
code benchmark and one CPU laptop. Training-set ex-
posure is unknown; benchmark familiarity is neither
ruled out nor inferred from a pass. Fixed task ros-
ters, noisy eligibility selection, one structural genera-
tion per cell and only three isolated stochastic samples
limit generalization. Shared functions connect M1 bun-
dles and prevent robust component-level intervals. The
smaller-model eﬀects need not predict larger models,
other quantizations, GPUs or cloud deployments.
The study is revised and partly exploratory. Reg-
istrations and dated amendments are preserved, but
the ﬁnal synthesis and cross-speciﬁcation audit are
posthoc. Local Git hashes support provenance, not
independent public timestamping. Family correction
does not erase prior exploration. The separate syn-
thetic prototype benchmark is small and templated,
with no user study.
Scoring depends on ﬁnite test suites, extraction and
a Windows restricted subprocess adapter. These con-
trols are documented rather than described as a com-
plete adversarial sandbox. Alternative construction
and similarity labels are AI-assisted and authored; rep-
resentative witnesses are not exhaustive semantic vali-
dation. Adverse censoring weakens the principal cross-
pass result. No recognition, internal state or inten-
tional giving-up measurement exists.
CPU-package estimates are not calibrated process-
speciﬁc energy. Unrelated work, shared baselines,
clock/sampling gaps, thermal eﬀects and approximate
post-load boundaries remain potential errors. Boot-
strap intervals reﬂect task variation, not measurement
calibration or independent block errors. Short requests
are particularly diﬃcult at 500 ms resolution. The in-
complete registered cache experiment prevents a com-
pleted primary energy claim.
11 Conclusion
Across seven studies, the useful distinction is between
supplying information and obtaining code that uses
it as intended. Cross-speciﬁcation testing makes one
class of failure visible: the 1.5B complete cases show
more alternative-consistent outputs under competing
context, although adverse censoring weakens the ﬁnd-
ing. Oracle retention has lower observed estimated en-
ergy per correct solution, while accuracy preservation
remains unresolved and policy eﬀects are confounded.
E5 supplies an equally important negative lesson: a
preference metric at its observed ﬂoor cannot support

evaluating memory selection, generated behavior and
total computation together. They do not establish an
internal memory mechanism or an integrated Memory-

Separate sandbox records cross-score 1,029 uncensored revised-interference outputs on their alternative suites,
including isolation and controls. Sixty capped per-function candidates and one merged complete-fence candidate

HumanEval/32’s original oracle anomaly is retained, with an exclusion sensitivity. Caps-as-missing and op-
timistic cap-pass scenarios preserve their diﬀerent denominators. Held-out eligibility sensitivity excludes the
selecting P1 sample0 from isolated-strategy comparison outcomes. The original all-task primary estimates re-

model knowledge.
The cross-pass complete-case comparison requires all four competing and irrelevant cells per task. Adverse/-
favorable assignments bound unknown indicators; their intervals describe assigned scenarios. The principal 1.5B

made. Zero both-pass cells remain explicit.
C Energy Robustness and the Partial Cache Diagnostic
Original block baselines, drift ﬂags and time/power windows remain in the export. Start, end and midpoint
subtraction are reported separately. Excluding drift or short windows changes the retained task population;
paired cost-ratio sensitivities require covered observations in both arms. Undeﬁned ratio draws are reported
rather than silently discarded. Total-request and approximate post-load estimates answer diﬀerent measurement
questions.
E4’s original feasibility pilot stops at 10 of 20 planned requests because one response ends short of its intended
workload. A separately registered JSON-array amendment completes a 20-call pilot, then saves 76 of 400 full
requests. These workloads and pilots are not pooled. The full run has 35 cold and 41 warm requests. Native
cached-token counters show zero for the cold requests and positive counts for the warm requests; model residency,
preparation and priming are logged separately. Matching by model, preﬁx and repeat yields only six pairs in

measured generation. No full E3 or U-family outcome is substituted.
